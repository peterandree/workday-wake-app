Why this page exists

On a stock Android device (a Google Pixel, for example), a well-written alarm app mostly just works. On a large share of the Android installed base it does not: several manufacturers ship power-management features that kill background apps, silently drop scheduled alarms, or block the app from restarting itself — usually without any notification to the app that something was cancelled.

The defining problem is that asymmetry: your alarm is gone, but the app is never told. Everything Workday Wake does about this is built around one question: if one mechanism gets wiped, which other mechanism notices?

This page is the public, readable version of that engineering story. It explains what these devices actually do, what the app does in response, and how you can see and share diagnostics from inside the app if something ever goes wrong. Per-brand setup steps and the app's own guidance screens come from the same source; if this page and the app ever disagree, the app's Settings > Reliability screen is the authoritative one for your device.

What a hostile environment actually does

Recents swipe-kill / force-stopXiaomi (MIUI/HyperOS), Samsung (One UI)Scheduled alarm registrations are wiped, with no callback to the app

Backup/restore and OS upgradesAll AndroidSame as a swipe-kill: registrations silently gone

The layers we stack

No single mechanism covers every skin above, so Workday Wake stacks independent layers. Each one assumes the previous layer may already have been destroyed:

PreventYour wake alarms are scheduled with setAlarmClock() — the highest-priority alarm API Android offers: exempt from Doze, visible in the system clock UI, and delivered even when the process is dead. It also puts the small alarm icon in the status bar; that is the app working, not a bug.
RestoreAlarms are re-registered after reboot, after timezone change, at every app start, and by periodic offline maintenance jobs (the exact-alarm permission is checked every 8 hours). Because these jobs do not depend on the process having survived, they can re-arm everything the OEM wiped.
SuperviseOn devices that need it, a small second process checks in with the main one every 6 hours. If a side has been silent for 13 hours, the other side re-arms the alarms. This layer is off by default (it costs memory); it switches on automatically for brands with known behaviour, or after the first recorded reliability incident, and it never silently switches off again.
Detect & healEvery alarm records when it was expected to fire; every actual ring records that it fired. If an expected alarm did not fire, the app counts a miss and silently re-schedules from the same source the alarm list uses — even while you were asleep.
Escalate honestlyIf detection itself appears to be failing (a guard has stayed silent for three consecutive checks), the app posts a single, visible notification telling you its alarm reliability is degraded — rather than pretending everything is fine.

If a layer exists only to catch the previous layer failing, it is doing its job — misses, self-heals and escalations are recorded, not hidden.

Per-brand setup steps (from the app's own guidance)

The app ships built-in guidance (Settings > Reliability on affected devices) with the exact steps per manufacturer. The relevant ones in short form — replace “Workday Wake” with the app name in your launcher wherever the setting names it:

Brand / skinSteps (as in the app's Device Battery Guide)
Xiaomi / MIUI, HyperOSAllow Autostart; in custom permissions enable “Show on Lock screen” and “Display pop-up windows while running in the background”; set App > Battery saver to “No restrictions”; lock the app in recent apps (swipe down or padlock).
Samsung / One UISettings > Battery and device care > Battery > Background usage limits: remove from “Sleeping apps” and “Deep sleeping apps”, add to “Never sleeping apps”; Settings > Apps > Workday Wake > Battery > Unrestricted. Also ensure “Put unused apps to sleep” is off — it can suppress alarms for apps unused for days.
Huawei / EMUI, HarmonyOSSettings > Battery > App launch: set “Manage manually” and enable Auto-launch, Secondary launch and Run in background; Battery optimization: “Don't allow” (opt out).
OnePlus / Oppo / Realme (ColorOS)Battery optimization: “Not optimized”; App info > Battery: “Allow background activity”/“Unrestricted”; in the startup manager allow auto-start; in DuraSpeed remove Workday Wake from the monitored list.
Vivo / iQOOIn the permissions manager allow auto-start; disable battery optimization; allow background activity and lock in recent apps.
SonySettings > Battery > STAMINA mode: “Off” — or turn off its “Prefer battery time” extra restrictor.

Further families (Asus PowerMaster, Meizu, Lenovo, Motorola Battery care, Nokia DuraSpeed, Transsion/itel/Infinix Phone Master) get the same treatment inside the app: Settings > Reliability detects your manufacturer and shows only the steps that apply to your device. All steps leave the app as permanent string resources; none are hard-coded or brand-specific forks.

Pro tip that works on nearly every skin: open Recent Apps and lock Workday Wake (padlock icon). This single step prevents most swipe-kill and memory-cleaner losses, and also fixes widgets that stop updating after you tap Skip.

Your on-device diagnostics

Every piece of reliability engineering above leaves a trace you can inspect locally. In Settings > Diagnostics the app answers the same questions a bug report would ask:

  • Website side: did the watchdog beat? when was the alarm last scheduled, when was it expected to fire, when did it actually fire?
  • How many misses were detected, and when did the app last self-heal (re-schedule after a miss)?
  • Your app version, Android version, manufacturer and model — where manufacturer-specific behaviour actually lives.
Everything on this screen is computed and stored on your device. Diagnostics is a mirror, not a transmitter: the app has no way to upload any of it by itself.

In Settings > Reliability you can also see the protection tier your device is on (“Tier 1 / Tier 2”), how many missed alarms were counted, and — on affected brands — the per-brand Device Battery Guide itself.

Sharing diagnostics — voluntary, on your terms

If an alarm failed and you want help, Settings > Diagnostics has a “send to developer” action:

  1. It is opt-in: a dialog lists every single field before anything happens.
  2. The report is technical only — timestamps, counters, version, manufacturer/model. No alarm labels, no names, no identifiers.
  3. You choose the destination (e-mail, messenger, anything with a share sheet). Nothing leaves the device unless you press send on that draft.
There is no upload endpoint, no crash-reporting service, no automatic background send anywhere in the app. A clipboard alternative exists if you would rather not use a share sheet at all.

You are in control

  • No accounts, no analytics, no advertising — reliability engineering is done with on-device resources only. See the privacy policy.
  • Alarm Guard: the persistent-notification service that keeps the app alive can be turned off in Settings > Reliability (at some cost to missed-alarm protection on restrictive devices).
  • Tier 2 monitoring only enables extra components after either a known brand match or a recorded incident; there is no hidden reporting channel.
  • Uninstall or clear app storage is a full reset: every diagnostic counter, heartbeat timestamp and missed-alarm record lives only in your device's app storage.

Honest limitations

  • Verified on an AOSP floor, not on every device. The resilience design is tested with Android Open Source Project primitives (process kills, deep Doze, standby buckets) on a throwaway Pixel AVD, because that is what can be automated. An emulator cannot fully replicate Xiaomi Autostart gates, Samsung Device Care, or OEM alarm throttling — real-device behaviour is approached via dontkillmyapp.com guidance, per-brand reports, and diagnostics sent by users like you.
  • No guarantees. Aggressive OEMs change their power management between firmware versions. If a brand introduces a new kill mechanism, the app may need an update to cover it; diagnostics is how we find out.
  • The alarm icon in the status bar is intentional — it comes with the scheduling API that serves your alarm best.
  • No iOS version exists. The engineering above depends on Android APIs (contract-based alarms, WorkManager, exact-alarm permission) that iOS does not offer comparable equivalents of.

Closing

An alarm clock has one job. Workday Wake was engineered from the start around the hardest versions of that job: it schedules in a way that survives process death, it re-arms what gets wiped, it supervises itself with a second process where needed, and it reports honestly on-device when something went wrong. And the privacy line is absolute — none of that honesty costs you your data.

Found a device where the guide does not hold? Please open an issue and, if you can, attach the on-device diagnostics via the voluntary share action. That feedback loop is the one thing an emulator cannot substitute.
